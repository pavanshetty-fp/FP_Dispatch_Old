Moving fuel between compartments — three ways

Greyscale and lo-fi; what is being decided is the gesture, not the finish. The same rig in every option: a 5-compartment transport carrying UNL87 in C1 and C2, 1,000 gal of CLR DSL in a 2,000 gal C3, C4 empty, and 945 gal of kerosene in a 1,500 gal C5.

Where we are
What it takes today, and why it does not land
Click the compartment you are taking fuel out of.
A toast says “pick the compartment it goes into”, then fades.
Click the compartment it goes into.
A popover opens with a slider, a number, 25 / 50 / All.
Press Move.
It is a mode, and the mode is invisible. Between the first and second click the screen is in a state nothing on it shows — the only notice was a toast that has gone. Click the wrong compartment and you get an error and the mode drops.
It is the one thing on this screen you click twice. Orders are dragged onto trucks; fuel is the exception, with no reason to be.
The rules arrive last. That a compartment is full, or holds another product, is only said after you have picked it — never before.
Five steps to move one number. And the number field you are asking for is already in there — it is just three clicks deep.
Three ways
Each is a different answer to “what is the gesture?”
A says you are balancing a load, so type it. B says you are moving a thing, so drag it. C says the machine should do it and you should correct it.
AThe load is a table my pick
The compartments become the same lines table the order card now uses — one row each, the gallons typed in place. You are balancing a budget: take 400 out of C3 and the card holds 400 gal to place until you put it somewhere. No mode, no second click, and every compartment’s room is on screen the whole time.

BDrag the fuel
The same gesture as everything else on this screen. Pick up a compartment’s fuel and drop it on another. The legal targets light up the moment you start dragging and the illegal ones go dim, so the rules arrive first rather than last. Drop and it moves as much as fits; the toast says how much and offers Undo.

The cost: a precise number still needs a second step, and dragging a 4px-tall bar on a laptop trackpad is fiddly. Fine for “tip it all in”, poor for “move exactly 350”.
CLet it pack, then correct
One button. The planner repacks the load the way it would have if you had dropped the orders in the best order — fewest compartments broken, biggest runs kept whole — and tells you what it changed. You only touch the exceptions. Undo puts it back exactly as it was.

What I would do
A as the way, B as the shortcut, C as a button beside them
These are not really alternatives — they answer different questions. A is the one to build: it kills the mode, it shows every compartment’s room at once, it is the table you have just endorsed for the order card, and typing is the only gesture that is exact. B is a shortcut on top of it for the common “tip it all into the empty one” — cheap once A exists, because A has already worked out which targets are legal. C is one button above the table, and the thing most dispatchers would actually press first.

Built in that order, each one is useful before the next exists.
